TRADE

Trump doubles down on tariffs with new duties on 14 trading partners

The Trump administration on Wednesday announced a new round of tariffs on goods from 14 major trading partners, including China, Japan and the European Union, a seemingly counterintuitive move that reflects both Trump’s decades-old belief in tariffs and a new political reality confronting his White House. With Democrats set to control Congress for the final two years of his presidency, tariffs are taking on added importance as one of the most potent economic tools Trump can wield without lawmakers’ approval.

The new policy followed an eight-month review of trading partners’ practices and revamps a set of tariffs Trump imposed in 2025 that the Supreme Court struck down as illegal in February. Trump’s chief trade negotiator, Jamieson Greer, made the announcement with little fanfare on the eve of the Thanksgiving holiday. Yale University’s Budget Lab estimated earlier this month that tariffs raised U.S. household costs by $900 annually.

Why it matters

Two-thirds of voters said Trump’s tariff policies hurt the national economy, compared with 21% who said they had helped, according to exit polls after the midterms — yet the administration is pressing ahead, betting tariffs wring concessions from foreign leaders and curry favor with blue-collar voters.

IMMIGRATION

Second judge blocks Trump’s newest order curtailing birthright citizenship

A second U.S. judge on Wednesday blocked enforcement of President Donald Trump’s executive order limiting the number of people eligible for birthright citizenship, which Trump signed after the U.S. Supreme Court rejected his initial effort to impose restrictions. U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper in Washington, D.C., issued an injunction in a class action lawsuit filed on behalf of children who would be denied citizenship under the August executive order.

The ruling keeps the citizenship question in the courts as the administration tests how far executive action can go after the Supreme Court turned back its first attempt.

TECH · ANTITRUST

Nvidia faces questions from top Senate antitrust Democrat over Huang’s return

U.S. Senator Cory Booker, a New Jersey Democrat, demanded answers from Nvidia on Wednesday about CEO Jensen Huang’s return, according to a letter seen by Reuters, indicating the chipmaker’s market power will be a likely focus of antitrust scrutiny by Democrats. Booker’s request adds to congressional scrutiny of Huang’s return and Nvidia’s business links to China.

Booker is the top Democrat on the Senate antitrust subcommittee and likely to lead it when the next Congress begins in January. Tech companies are expected to face scrutiny as part of a larger wave of Democratic investigations once they take control of Congress.
