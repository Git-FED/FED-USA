SANCTIONS · UKRAINE

Trump waives sweeping Russia sanctions; Zelenskiy calls the move “wrong”

President Donald Trump signed an executive order on Friday night waiving the sanctions, restrictions and duties in the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, blocking penalties that were due to take effect Sunday under a law he signed just a month earlier. The order invokes Section 115 of the law, which lets the president waive its provisions after certifying to Congress that doing so serves the national interest. The blocked measures included a 500 percent duty on Russian goods and 100 percent duties on the biggest buyers of Russian oil and gas.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskiy said the decision “definitely helps only the Russians,” noting Russian forces struck more than 15 power generation and transmission sites on Friday. Republican Senator Thom Tillis called the waiver “beyond irresponsible,” Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer called it “giving the Russian economy life support,” and Representative Thomas Massie defended it, noting Trump used authority Congress itself granted. Moscow welcomed the move.

Why it matters

The Graham Act passed the Senate 86-11 and was designed to remove presidential discretion from sanctions — but its own Section 115 waiver turned out to be the escape hatch. If Congress does not fix the law, future sanctions bills may trade at a discount, since the trigger they fear can be disarmed by a presidential certification.

TRADE

Trading partners answer new U.S. tariff proposals with anger, protest and diplomacy

Major U.S. trading partners responded with disappointment and protests while holding out the prospect of negotiations after the Trump administration rolled out fresh Section 301 tariffs on goods from China, the European Union and a dozen other economies. China said it reserved the right to take necessary measures; the EU rejected the claim that Europe causes overcapacity and urged Washington to honor the 15% tariff ceiling in their existing deal. Japan called the plan “regrettable,” and Singapore said it disagreed with the findings.

The proposed duties follow an eight-month Section 301 investigation into structural excess capacity. China would face up to 20%, potentially 40% if the trade truce falls apart; the EU 15%, potentially 20%; Vietnam up to 35%. Written comments are due January 5, 2027, with hearings starting January 25.

Why it matters

Two-thirds of voters said Trump’s tariff policies hurt the national economy, compared with 21% who said they had helped, according to exit polls after the midterms — yet the administration is pressing ahead, betting tariffs wring concessions from foreign leaders and curry favor with blue-collar voters.

TECH · ANTITRUST

S&P puts Nvidia’s AA rating on watch over governance after Huang’s return

S&P Global Ratings placed Nvidia’s AA credit rating on CreditWatch with negative implications, warning it could downgrade the company within 90 days unless concerns about corporate governance are addressed. The agency cited board independence, oversight of senior executives, succession planning and questions about potential export-control violations after CEO Jensen Huang resigned on November 12 and was reinstated on November 23. S&P expects any cut to be limited to one notch; Moody’s held its Aa1 rating steady.

The warning follows Senator Cory Booker’s demand for answers from Nvidia about Huang’s return. Booker, the top Democrat on the Senate antitrust subcommittee, signaled the chipmaker’s market power will be a likely focus of antitrust scrutiny by Democrats once they take control of Congress in January.
