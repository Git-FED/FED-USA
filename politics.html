TRUMP · CONGRESS

Trump says veto power is a starting point for Democrats

President Donald Trump said Friday he will veto legislation passed by Democrats when they take control of Congress in 2027, but said his veto power should be seen as a starting point for lawmakers who want change. Trump said many Democrats had called him about healthcare and immigration, and pointed to healthcare as an area where both sides might meet in the middle.

Trump also said Democratic Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa had called him about the border, and he thought they could find a solution. With the new Congress set to choose its leadership soon, Trump described Senator Chuck Schumer, vying to become Senate majority leader, and Representative Hakeem Jeffries, on the path to becoming House speaker, as New Yorkers he could work with.

Why it matters

The veto matters because Democrats take control of Congress in January 2027, so Trump’s signature becomes the gate for their agenda. Healthcare is the area he named where a deal might be possible; immigration and the border were the other topics he said Democrats had raised with him.

OVERSIGHT

Democrats put Trump administration on notice over alleged document destruction

Democrats and the White House are already in a showdown over alleged document destruction — and Democrats have not even taken control of Congress yet. Democratic lawmakers sent preservation letters across the federal government demanding officials properly save documents and turn off auto-delete settings on encrypted messaging apps.

The White House said Democrats are making claims about document destruction without evidence, but also indicated it sees no obligation to comply with preservation demands before Democrats gain committee gavels in January. Democrats say the letters draw a line in the sand, so that if records are destroyed, those responsible could face prosecution for obstruction of Congress.

SENATE

Senate passes permitting overhaul 62-33

The Senate voted 62-33 on Thursday to pass the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, which overhauls permitting for energy and infrastructure projects. The bill now goes to the House, where it is expected to clear after Thanksgiving.

The bill is designed to cut permitting timelines and make new limits on the duration of lawsuits and the scope of reviews under the Clean Water Act and National Environmental Policy Act, as lawmakers seek to increase energy supplies to meet surging demand and lower electricity prices.
